# Screen Time Video: Discussion Guide

**Video:** "Solving Screen Addiction for Kids & Teens — Media Stamped #1" by Nicole Stamp (Global Citizen, 13:21)
**URL:** https://youtu.be/6iJdRoeYay0
**Audience:** 11-year-old twin boys
**Purpose:** Watch together, pause at chapter markers, use these questions to check understanding and spark conversation. The goal is internalization, not memorization.

---

## How to Use This Guide

Watch the video WITH them. The video has chapter markers built in. Pause at each chapter and ask the questions below. Don't frame it as a test — frame it as "let's see if we both got that."

The goal is not getting the right answer. The goal is getting them to think about the concepts in their own words.

---

## Discussion Points (by chapter)

### 0:00-0:40 — Intro

**Key concept:** By the time you are 12, you have probably spent an entire year of your life online.

**Questions to ask:**
- "She says by age 12, you've spent about a whole year of your life online. Does that sound right to you? Does it feel like a lot or a little?"
- "What do you think you were doing during that year? Games, videos, messaging?"

**What to listen for:** This is a warm-up. The "one year of your life" stat is a hook — it gets them thinking about the scale of their screen use without feeling accused.

---

### 0:41-2:25 — "Are You Actually Addicted?"

**Key concept:** Addiction means doing something so much it causes negative effects but you still can't stop. Screen time is not chemically addictive like drugs, but it can cause real problems. The video includes a 6-question self-quiz.

**The 6 questions (have them answer honestly — out loud or on paper):**
1. Do you spend a lot of time thinking about screen time or planning what you'll do online?
2. Do you find you want to use screen time more and more?
3. Do you use screen time to help you forget about your problems?
4. Do you try to cut down on screen time but it doesn't work?
5. When you can't have screen time, do you feel restless or upset?
6. Does screen time cause problems with your family, relationships, or schoolwork?

**Scoring:** One point for each "yes." More than 3 out of 6 means screen time might be causing problems.

**Questions to ask after the quiz:**
- "How did you score? Be honest — I'm not going to be mad either way."
- "Which questions were the easiest to say yes to?"
- "Do you think the quiz is fair, or is it rigged to make everyone look addicted?"

**What to listen for:** The key is whether they answer honestly, not whether they score high or low. If they get defensive or say "no" to everything, that is a signal. If they are honest — even if they score a 5 or 6 — that is a huge win. It means they are self-aware, which is the foundation of self-regulation.

**Important:** Take the quiz yourself too. Show them your own score. This models honesty and removes the "you're just lecturing me" dynamic.

---

### 2:26-4:55 — Dopamine and Adrenaline

**Key concept:** Dopamine is a brain chemical that feels good — it is a reward for things that help you survive (finding food, solving a problem, making eye contact with someone you like). Screens give huge amounts of dopamine, especially scrolling — every new thing you see gives a little burst. Adrenaline is the fight-or-flight chemical. In the past, you burned it off by literally fighting or running. Now you get adrenaline from screens but just sit there, so it builds up and can make you angry or anxious.

**Questions to ask:**
- "She says dopamine is like a reward your brain gives you. What kinds of things give you dopamine hits?"
- "She mentions getting adrenaline from a 1v5 situation in a game. Have you ever felt that? Your heart racing, muscles tensing? What happened after the game ended — did that energy go away or did you still feel wired?"
- "She says our prehistoric ancestors burned off adrenaline by fighting or running. But when we get it from screens, we just sit there. Where does that energy go? Do you ever feel cranky or restless after gaming?"

**What to listen for:** The adrenaline concept is important and often overlooked. If they can connect "I feel wound up after gaming" to "my body is full of adrenaline I didn't burn off," that is a genuine insight. It reframes the crankiness after screen time as a physiological response, not a character flaw.

---

### 4:56-7:47 — Intermittent Rewards

**Key concept:** This is the slot machine mechanism. If every scroll gave you something amazing, you would get bored. If every scroll gave you nothing, you would quit. But if you get something good at unpredictable intervals — every 3rd, 7th, or 2nd scroll — you stay hooked. This is called intermittent reinforcement. The puppy example: you perked up when you saw the puppy because it was a surprise. The surprise itself triggers a bigger dopamine release.

**Questions to ask:**
- "She shows a puppy as a surprise. Why did a surprise puppy give you more dopamine than if I told you 'look at this puppy' and showed you one?"
- "How is scrolling like a slot machine? What is the 'reward' when you scroll?"
- "She says the rewards come 'every 3rd, 7th, or 2nd time' — unpredictable. Why is unpredictable better than predictable for keeping you hooked? If you knew exactly when the next good thing would show up, would you scroll the same way?"

**What to listen for:** If they can articulate "I keep scrolling because I don't know when something good will show up, and that unpredictability is what keeps me going," they understand intermittent reinforcement. Try this: "If YouTube showed you only one good video every 20 videos, and told you which one it was, would you watch the other 19?" This makes the concept concrete.

---

### 7:48-9:14 — Long-Term Impacts

**Key concept:** Cutting screen time from 3 hours to 1 hour per day can improve self-esteem, sleep, and focus in as little as 21 days. The first few days feel like you are "going to melt from boredom" but it gets better.

**Questions to ask:**
- "She says cutting from 3 hours to 1 hour can improve how you feel in 3 weeks. Does that seem realistic to you? Does 3 weeks feel long or short?"
- "She says the first few days feel like 'melting from boredom.' Why do you think that happens? What is your brain doing during that boredom?"
- "Has anyone ever told you that boredom is bad? Do you think boredom can actually be useful?"

**What to listen for:** The boredom point is critical. If they can connect boredom to their brain adjusting (dopamine reset), they understand that the discomfort is temporary and meaningful. If they think "boredom is just bad and I should avoid it," that is the exact mindset that drives excessive screen use. Redirect to: "What if boredom is your brain's way of processing and resetting? What if it is actually doing something important?"

---

### 9:15-13:21 — Tips: Seven "Level Ups"

**Key concept:** Seven practical strategies to take control of screen time, framed as "level ups" (gaming language).

**The 7 level ups:**
1. No screens in the bedroom
2. Try screen-free mornings
3. Set your phone screen to grayscale
4. Hide your phone out of sight
5. Plan screen-free hangouts
6. Use screens better — connect with people, practice hobbies, learn skills (not just scroll alone)
7. Turn off notifications on social media and gaming apps

**Questions to ask:**
- "Which of these level ups do you think would be the hardest for you? Why?"
- "Which ones do you already do?"
- "She says you don't have to use screens less — just use them better. What does that mean? Can you give an example of 'better' screen use vs. 'worse' screen use?"
- "If you had to pick TWO level ups to try this week, which would you pick?"

**What to listen for:** The "use screens better, not just less" message is important — it aligns with your goal. They are not being asked to quit screens. They are being asked to be intentional. If they can distinguish "playing a game with my friend" from "scrolling alone for an hour," they understand the difference.

**The grayscale tip is worth highlighting:** Setting a phone/tablet to grayscale makes it less visually stimulating, which reduces the dopamine trigger. It is a concrete, reversible experiment they can try immediately.

---

## After the Video: The Big Question

Once the discussion is done, ask the question that ties it all together:

"You just learned that your brain gives you dopamine hits every time you scroll, that the unpredictability of rewards keeps you hooked like a slot machine, that adrenaline from gaming builds up and makes you cranky, and that cutting back can make you feel better in 3 weeks. Knowing all that — what do YOU think is a reasonable amount of screen time for you?"

Let them answer. Do not correct them immediately. If they say "4 hours," ask "Okay, and how would you feel after 4 hours, based on what the video said?" Let them work through it.

Then ask: "Which two level ups do you want to try this week?"

Write down what they come up with. That becomes the starting point for the agreement you build together. This is where the self-regulation goal lives — the video gives them the information, the discussion makes sure they understood it, and this question asks them to USE it.
