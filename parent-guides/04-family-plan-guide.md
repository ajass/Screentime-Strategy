# The Family Plan: Parent Guide

**Purpose:** How to handle the negotiation, build the agreement, and manage the transition when your kids are obsessed with their tablets.

---

## The Core Tension

You built this entire approach around self-regulation and giving them agency. If they propose a number and you override it, you become the authoritarian parent the whole framework was designed to avoid. But you also cannot let 4 hours a day go unchallenged when the research shows 4+ hours is exactly where negative health effects begin.

The solution is not to reject their number. It is to create a structure where their number gets tested against reality and they adjust it themselves.

---

## If They Propose a Number That Is Too High (e.g., 4 hours summer / 2 hours school)

### Do NOT say:
- "That is too much."
- "No, you are getting X hours."
- "You are addicted and do not know what is good for you."

Any of these undermines the entire framework. You become the authority, they become the subject, and the self-regulation goal is dead.

### DO say:
- "Okay, let's look at that. The video said 4+ hours is where the negative effects start — sleep problems, anxiety, depression, losing interest in other things. You are right at that line. Do you think you would notice those effects at 4 hours?"
- "Let's try your number for two weeks. But here is the deal — we are going to check in at the end of each week. You tell me honestly: how is your sleep? Are you still doing other things you enjoy? How do you feel after 4 hours on the screen vs. after 1 hour? If the answers are bad, we renegotiate. If they are fine, we keep it."
- "I am not going to override your number. You earned the right to propose it by doing the work. But I am going to hold you accountable for checking whether it is actually working for you."

### Why this works:
- It respects their agency
- It uses the research they just learned as the measuring stick, not your authority
- It builds in a review period so the number is not permanent
- It teaches them to self-monitor, which is the actual goal
- If 4 hours is genuinely too much, they will feel it — and the video told them exactly what to look for

### The likely outcome:
At 11, completely obsessed with tablets, they will probably propose something high. That is expected. The first two weeks will feel fine to them because they are getting what they want. But the check-in questions will start to surface real information: "Are you sleeping okay?" "Have you done anything besides screens this week?" "Do you feel better or worse after 4 hours than after 1?"

The goal is not to get the right number on day one. It is to build the feedback loop that gets them there over time.

## Structuring the Negotiation

### Step 1: Accept their number as a starting point, not a final answer

Write it down. Do not negotiate yet. Say: "This is your starting number. We will test it for two weeks and then review."

### Step 2: Define what counts as screen time

This is critical and must be agreed on during the negotiation, not imposed later:

**Counts as screen time:**
- Games (solo or with friends online)
- YouTube, TikTok, scrolling
- Social media, messaging apps used for browsing/scrollling
- Watching shows/movies on the tablet

**Does NOT count as screen time:**
- Homework on a device (school-assigned)
- Reading a book on a device (if it is actually reading, not switching to games)
- Video calls with family
- Coding, art, or creative apps used to MAKE something (not consume)

The distinction is consumption vs. creation. Consuming content is screen time. Creating something or doing schoolwork is not. This is worth discussing with them — it teaches them to think about WHAT they are doing on the screen, not just HOW LONG.

### Step 3: Set the structure around the number

The number is not the whole plan. You also need:

**Screen-free zones (non-negotiable):**
- Bedrooms — tablets charge in the kitchen or living room overnight
- Dinner table
- Car (unless it is a long trip — that is a separate conversation)

**Screen-free times (non-negotiable):**
- First 30 minutes after waking up
- Last hour before bed (this is the most important one — screen time before bed disrupts sleep, and sleep is where the brain repairs and consolidates memory)
- During family activities (hikes, outings, etc.)

**Within the allowed time, they control what they do:**
- This is their time. If they want to spend 2 hours gaming with friends, that is their choice. If they want to watch YouTube, that is their choice. The point is that the time is bounded, not that you control the content within it.

### Step 4: Build in the review mechanism

**Weekly check-in (5 minutes, one-on-one, not together):**
Ask each boy privately:
1. "How was your sleep this week? Did you fall asleep okay?"
2. "Did you do anything besides screens that you enjoyed?"
3. "How did you feel after screen time — better or worse?"
4. "Did you ever find it hard to stop when your time was up?"

Write down their answers. Do not judge. Just collect data.

**Two-week review (family meeting, all together):**
- Look at the data from the weekly check-ins
- Ask: "Is your number working for you?"
- If yes: keep it
- If no: "What number do you want to try for the next two weeks?"
- Adjust and repeat

This is not a one-time negotiation. It is an ongoing conversation. The number will change over time — lower in summer, maybe lower as they build other interests, maybe higher on special occasions. The point is that THEY are driving the adjustment based on self-observation, not you imposing changes.

## Managing the Transition (They Are Obsessed)

If they are completely obsessed, cutting back will be hard regardless of what number you agree on. The first 1-3 weeks will be rough. Here is what to expect and how to handle it:

### What to expect:
- Boredom complaints ("there is nothing to do")
- Irritability and mood swings (dopamine withdrawal is real)
- Pushing boundaries (trying to sneak extra time, negotiating in the moment)
- Testing whether you will cave

### What to do:
**1. Stock the offline environment BEFORE you start.**
Have things ready: board games, art supplies, sports equipment, library books, a list of friends they can call to hang out. If they hit boredom and there is nothing to do, they will fight for the tablet. If there is an alternative, the transition is easier.

**2. Do not engage in the moment.**
When they are upset about stopping, that is the dopamine talking. Do not argue, do not explain, do not negotiate. Say: "I know it is hard to stop. Your time is up. We can talk about it later." Walk away. The urge passes in 2-5 minutes — the video taught this. Let it pass.

**3. Model it yourself.**
If you are on your phone during family time, the whole thing collapses. Be honest: "I am putting my phone away too. This is hard for adults also." Naming it builds credibility.

**4. Expect relapse.**
They will have good days and bad days. A bad day does not mean the plan failed. It means they are 11 and their brains are still developing. Reset and try again the next day. Do not pile on consequences for slip-ups — the natural consequence (feeling worse after) is more instructive than a punishment.

**5. Use the "wanting vs. liking" language.**
When they are pushing for more time, ask: "Are you wanting this or are you enjoying it?" This uses the exact concept from the video. It plants the question in their head. They may not answer honestly in the moment, but it builds the self-awareness muscle.

**6. Enrich their offline life.**
The NPR research showed that when offline life gets richer, screen cravings diminish on their own. Not because screens are banned, but because there is something better. Plan activities, invite friends over, go places, build things. This is the long game — the more compelling their real life is, the less they need the tablet.

## When to Adjust Your Approach

The self-regulation approach assumes the child has the capacity to self-monitor and adjust. At 11, they are developing that capacity but they are not there yet. If after 4-6 weeks of genuine effort:

- They are not being honest in the check-ins
- They are sneaking devices or finding workarounds
- They are not doing anything offline
- Their sleep, mood, or schoolwork is getting worse, not better
- They cannot articulate any self-awareness about their screen use

...then the self-regulation approach needs supplementation. This does not mean abandoning it — it means adding structure:

- Use parental controls to enforce the agreed-upon time limit (do this WITH them, not secretly: "You agreed to 2 hours. I am setting the tablet to lock at 2 hours. This is not a punishment — it is a tool to help you keep the agreement you made.")
- Reduce the review period to weekly instead of biweekly
- Consider professional support (CBT has been shown effective for this age group)

The goal is still self-regulation. The controls are scaffolding, not a permanent solution. As they demonstrate they can self-regulate, the controls come off.

## The Consumption vs. Creation Loophole

Kids who are addicted will rationalize consumption as connection or creation to stay in the dopamine loop. "I'm texting a friend" while actually scrolling. "I'm making a video" while actually watching YouTube. The distinction between consumption and creation is sound in theory but exploitable in practice.

This does not mean the distinction is wrong. It means you verify, you do not take their word for it. Not because you do not trust them, but because the video taught both of you that dopamine makes people want things even when they know they should stop. Rationalization is a dopamine behavior, not a character flaw.

### How to handle it:

**1. Check the tablet's screen time report.**

Both iPad Screen Time and Android Family Link show per-app usage. If they claim 2 hours was "texting friends," look at the report. If Messages was 15 minutes and YouTube was 1 hour 45 minutes, you have data, not an argument.

"I noticed the report says YouTube was 1:45 and Messages was 15 minutes. Let's talk about that."

No accusation — just the data.

**2. If they claim "creation mode," ask for output.**

Creation produces output. If they spent 2 hours "making a video" and there is no video, that was consumption. If they were coding, show me the code. If they were drawing, show me the drawing. Creation leaves evidence. Consumption does not.

This is not a gotcha — it is the natural definition of the distinction.

**3. Cap the messaging loophole.**

Messaging is excluded from the limit because real connection is healthy. But if they are using 3 of their 4 hours "texting," that is not connection — that is scrolling with a messaging app open. Set a reasonable cap: messaging is excluded up to 30 minutes. Beyond that, it counts toward screen time. Most parental controls let you set a time limit on Messages specifically.

**What to say:**

"The agreement says consumption counts and creation does not. If you are creating, show me what you created. If you are connecting, 30 minutes of texting is plenty for real conversation. If you need 3 hours of texting, that is not connection — that is scrolling. The video explained the difference between wanting and enjoying. This is the same thing."

## How to Know It Is Working: Success Indicators

If you do not define success, you will never know whether the plan is working or just existing. Success in this framework is not a specific number of hours. It is a set of behavioral indicators that show self-regulation developing. The right amount of time is the number at which all of these indicators are positive. If any are negative, the number is too high.

### Sleep (the most objective measure)
- Are they falling asleep within 15-20 minutes of going to bed?
- Are they waking up rested, or groggy and resistant?
- Are they sleeping through the night?
- Is bedtime consistent, or are they bargaining for "just 5 more minutes" on the tablet?

Sleep is the canary. If sleep degrades, the number is too high or the screen-free-before-bed rule is not being enforced. You do not need to ask them about sleep — you can observe it.

### Offline engagement (the displacement measure)
- Are they doing anything besides screens voluntarily, without being told to?
- Are they initiating offline activities on their own (going outside, building something, calling a friend to hang out)?
- Or do they default to the tablet whenever there is unstructured time?

If the answer to the third question is "always the tablet," the offline environment is not rich enough or the screen time is still crowding everything else out. When offline life gets richer, screen craving diminishes on its own. If it is not diminishing, the offline life needs more investment.

### Mood after screen time (the dopamine measure)
- After they stop, are they content and ready to move on, or irritable and restless?
- Do they feel better or worse than before they started?
- Is the transition off the tablet smooth or a battle every time?

This is the Goldilocks test from the video. If they feel worse after, the activity is in the compulsive loop. If they feel better, it is healthy dopamine. You ask this in the weekly check-in, but you also observe it.

### Ability to stop (the self-regulation measure)
- Can they stop when the timer goes off without a meltdown?
- Are they starting to stop BEFORE the timer goes off?
- Can they articulate what is happening when it is hard to stop ("I know I should stop but I want to keep going")?

The progression is: fights the timer, tolerates the timer, anticipates the timer, stops without the timer. Where they are on this spectrum tells you whether self-regulation is developing.

### Honesty (the trust measure)
- Are they truthful in the check-ins?
- Are they reporting usage accurately, or are you finding discrepancies with the tablet's usage report?
- Are they hiding screen time or sneaking devices?

Honesty is not about the screen time itself — it is about whether the framework is working. If they are lying, they are not self-monitoring, which means the scaffolding needs to increase, not decrease.

### Self-awareness (the ultimate measure)
- Can they tell the difference between "I am enjoying this" and "I cannot stop"?
- Do they ever self-initiate stopping? ("I have been on for a while, I should do something else.")
- Can they name what is happening when they are in the dopamine loop?

This is the goal. When a kid can say "I know I should stop but my brain does not want to" — that is self-awareness. It does not mean they can stop yet, but it means they understand what is happening, which is the prerequisite for eventually being able to stop.

### How to know the number is right

The number is right when:
- Sleep is solid
- They are doing offline things without being forced
- Mood after screen time is neutral or positive
- They can stop without a meltdown
- Check-ins are honest
- They show some self-awareness about the dopamine loop

The number is too high when:
- Sleep degrades
- They do nothing but screens when unstructured
- Mood after screen time is irritable or flat
- Stopping is a battle every time
- They are lying or sneaking

The number is too low (less common but possible) when:
- They are compliant but resentful
- They feel punished rather than supported
- They stop easily but are bored and miserable offline with nothing to fill the gap

If the number is too low, the fix is not more screen time — it is more offline options. A bored kid does not need more tablet. He needs something to do.

### How to know the activities are right

Check the tablet's per-app usage report weekly. The healthy distribution looks like:
- A mix of messaging friends (connection) and gaming with friends (social play)
- Some creative app use that produces output (coding, art, video)
- Some entertainment consumption (YouTube, shows) within the limit
- No single app dominating 80%+ of the time

The unhealthy distribution looks like:
- One consumption app (YouTube, TikTok, a single game) dominating most of the time
- Zero creative app use
- Messaging used as a cover for scrolling
- Usage that spikes when they are stressed, upset, or bored — using screens as the only coping mechanism

The question is not just how much time — it is what the time is made of. 2 hours of gaming with friends is different from 2 hours of solo TikTok scrolling. The same number can be healthy or unhealthy depending on the mix.

### Realistic timeline

Do not expect all indicators to be positive in week 1.

- Weeks 1-2: sleep may get worse before it gets better (dopamine withdrawal can disrupt sleep). Mood will be rough. This is normal.
- Weeks 3-4: sleep should start improving. Mood after screen time should start normalizing. Pushback at the timer should decrease.
- Weeks 5-8: offline engagement should start appearing — not every day, but sometimes. They should be able to stop without a full meltdown most days.
- Months 3-4: self-awareness should be emerging. They should be able to name the dopamine loop sometimes. They might start stopping before the timer.
- Months 5-6: the number may be stable. Check-ins should be honest. The tablet is a tool, not a lifeline.

If you are not seeing improvement in the indicators by week 6, something in the system needs adjustment — the number, the controls, the offline environment, or the check-in process. That is not failure. It is calibration.

Success is not "they use screens the right amount." Success is "they can tell when they have had enough and can choose to stop." That takes months. The number is scaffolding for that skill, not the skill itself.

---

## Key Principles to Remember

1. The number is not the point. The feedback loop is the point. A kid who self-monitors at 3 hours is healthier than a kid who is forced to stop at 1 hour and resents it.

2. Expect the number to start high and come down over time as they build self-awareness. This is a months-long process, not a one-conversation fix.

3. The hardest part is not setting the limit. It is maintaining it consistently when they push back. Every time you cave, you teach them that pushing back works. Every time you hold the line calmly and kindly, you teach them that boundaries are real.

4. Their obsession is not a character flaw. It is a neural pathway being exploited by systems designed to exploit it. The video taught them this. Your job is to help them build the counter-pathway.

5. This is a family project. If you and Denise are not aligned, the kids will exploit the gap. Have the conversation about your own screen habits before you have the conversation with them.
